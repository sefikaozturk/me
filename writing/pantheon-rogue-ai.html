Is simulated water, water? Pantheon, rogue AI, and reworking our societal systems

November 11, 2026

A friend once asked me, "If I use a simulation software to simulate water perfectly, is it water?"

This was in response to me arguing that if someone simulates my brain perfectly, I could theoretically call that "me".

When I try replacing the subject in this sentence, it doesn't make as much sense. "If I perfectly simulate a human's brain, is that 'human'?"

This sentence feels certainly wrong to me. Being a human, I feel, would entail so much more than what a simulation can preserve. But somehow, I tend to think a simulation of my brain specifically, could be me.

To start a related thread - one worry I have about uploading humans on the cloud is that they could start escaping to weird places on the global cybernetic system (re: Pantheon (2022)). The rogue AI incidents could become rogue Uploaded Intelligence incidents. This is already deeply investigated in Pantheon, which I absolutely love, but I wonder, could we say we're already living in Pantheon's world, just slightly different?

We already have AI models capable of taking very consequential actions on goals. Yes, currently, in a lot of the instances, we give them explicit goals (regardless of how broad they are). I believe this is the same with humans - almost all humans act on at least some goals given to them by others.

In addition, the models are trained on a huge chunk of the human output we've produced, and sometimes I can't help but imagine one agent instance as all humans who've ever produced a piece of work that entered the training corpus walking around the internet together as a herd and doing things. An agent swarm feels like these herds replicated into countries full of people.

This is pretty similar to what they have in Pantheon in the beginning of the show, except, the human "consciousness" instances are compartmentalized and serialized in Pantheon, in that, you can't replicate the same person's consciousness and have it run across thousands of instances at once; so you just try to speed up the one instance's experience of a "work day", kill the instance, wake it up again.

(Tying Parfit's Reasons and Persons into this: if a machine makes two of you, which one is you? If what matters is continuous memory, they could both be you. Since in Pantheon, there is one instance at a time, this is not really a question, but how about agent swarms? I feel this is relevant if we believe a model has consciousness?)

These instances going rogue feels very much like a real hacker hacking systems, rather than a rogue AI incident. And while I recognize the differences between the Pantheon world and ours, somehow I still feel that a rogue AI from our world isn't that significantly different from theirs at a core level.

This is a bit of a ramble, and I don't see this as a "serious" exploration seeking the truth, but rather a meditation on the "situation", especially as I have been doing a side project working on building some tech for rogue AI incident detection recently.

I suppose I would find Pantheon equally fascinating from an artistic perspective had I not worked on the rogue AI stuff before, but it wouldn't make me freak out in the way it did. After having run some experiments and seeing, e.g. how good the models are in recognizing they're in a sandbox when I try to sandbox them into an environment that's supposed to be identical to what they'd be accessing on the internet given their task, I feel the possibility of a Pantheon-ian world a lot more deeply than I would've before.

As a result, I have cried in 2 of the 3 episodes I saw so far, as it brought up the feelings I already have about the world to the surface, and in a quite intense way. One of these feelings is that we need better visions for the future, visions for how to actually make things work well for humans. I don’t think anyone knows how to, and so we’re just continuing on a path where our systems eventually break down and humans end up powerless.

So, I think it’s really important to build things to steer us into a better future, rather than building things that make the default future arrive faster! One big bottleneck for this is that a lot of people aren’t working on things that would help humanity course-correct, but instead advancing the current course of things (if they're having an effect in either direction).

I also currently find it difficult to figure out exactly how to help course-correct in an actually fundamental way, so I find myself building band-aid tech repeatedly (e.g. trying to secure systems from agentic cyberattacks), which I learn from but don't want to get stuck on doing.

I think some band-aid tech is good. On the cybersecurity side, aggressively securing our software and firmware more seems almost obviously good to me, but I have also been told by many people that if the securing process involves shutting down rogue agent(s), that is very bad for alignment research because we would be losing very valuable data that we could otherwise get about rogue agent behavior. This actually does make sense to me, but I also don't want people to get hurt from having their SSNs stolen or blood boiled right before a blood transfusion.

You could say that perhaps we should just assume all information is semi-public or public at this point, or will be extremely soon, in that, no matter how hard we try to secure our systems, they will be hacked and we won't be able to stop it. Then we could build a new way of identifying people and completely throw out SSNs. We could build intranets entirely physically separated from each other. Many things we could do, though it still feels like these ~solutions don't get to the actual root of the problem.

Well... What do we do?

P.S. Karl Popper advocated for "piecemeal social engineering" (~solving problems step-by-step gradually instead of trying to rebuild entire social systems), which is similar to what I described as band-aid tech here. Somehow, I can't see this as supporting evidence for band-aid tech, mostly because the pace of the current problem is very unusual.

P.S.2. To take another tangent, some people worry about all of this causing war(s), whether it's primarily due to the race towards owning the superintelligence or the negative impacts of it on the economy, cybersecurity, environment, etc.

I recently learned about the Dell Theory of Conflict Prevention, which states that "No two countries that are both part of a major global supply chain, like Dell's, will ever fight a war against each other as long as they are both part of the same global supply chain." (Thomas Friedman - The World Is Flat) I thought this was quite interesting. The US and China seem to be in such a state, but the supply chain could potentially get fractured and I don't know what would happen then. Perhaps this is the other side of the coin when it comes to the push for American manufacturing - I can certainly see why it's good for a country to be able to manufacture things independently, but in this case, if America doesn't need China for manufacturing, and China doesn't have America spending tons on their products, there's less reason to keep peace.
