Polarizing ideas, the Antichrist, AI safety

November 2026

Peter Thiel is a very polarizing figure. When he was giving lectures in Rome, there was an article published about him, titled "Should we burn Peter Thiel?"

I was at Peter Thiel's Antichrist Lectures last month, and quickly saw that even just engaging with his ideas, let alone agreeing or supporting them, was very polarizing in a lot of the social circles I'm in.

Still, I got very nerdsniped by Thiel. Primarily, his epistemology and the way he deals with truth - for example, he hedges his bets by believing in multiple things at once, takes many contradicting actions knowingly, and that is very interesting to me.

This is the same man whose famous interview question is "What important truth do very few people agree with you on?" His whole worldview is built on the idea that the consensus is usually wrong. Someone who distrusts consensus that much has a good reason not to commit to any single truth either.

I also understand the arguments against him. Palantir is building mass surveillance technologies while Thiel preaches that a totalitarian world government will lead to the arrival of Antichrist. A mass surveillance system directly aids a totalitarian regime!

My friend who went to work for Palantir and I had a lot of discussions about whether Palantir is evil. He believes Palantir is morally neutral since if Palantir didn't do what they do, someone else would do it. I don't think this is a valid reason to justify a morally neutral stance, but I also have a difficult time believing in Palantir outright being an "evil" organization.

What makes an organization evil, versus an individual?

Reinhold Niebuhr wrote a whole book on this, Moral Man and Immoral Society. His argument was that groups are capable of things the individuals inside them would never do alone, because no single person feels responsible for the whole. Hannah Arendt called bureaucracy "the rule of Nobody," and she wrote that "the sad truth is that most evil is done by people who never make up their minds to be good or evil." "If we didn't do it, someone else would" is exactly the sentence you say when you haven't made up your mind. It doesn't make my friend evil. It makes him a part of something no one in particular is steering.

What's the limit of colliding what a person does and what they think? How much can a person be compartmentalized? Can you admire a dictator's art?

Orwell had an answer about Salvador Dalí: "One ought to be able to hold in one's head simultaneously the two facts that Dalí is a good draughtsman and a disgusting human being." But a painting doesn't do anything. The question gets harder when the person's ideas are not on a canvas but in a company, and the company is building the thing the ideas warn against.

Can you hold two beliefs at once and hedge your bets? Or is this just stupid?

F. Scott Fitzgerald thought it was the opposite of stupid: "The test of a first-rate intelligence is the ability to hold two opposed ideas in mind at the same time and still retain the ability to function." Keats called it negative capability, being "capable of being in uncertainties, mysteries, doubts, without any irritable reaching after fact and reason." But Robert Aumann proved that two rational people with the same priors can't agree to disagree. So when one person holds two contradicting beliefs, it's either genuine uncertainty or a desire to have it both ways. Hume would say it's usually the second: "Reason is, and ought only to be the slave of the passions." I'm still not sure which one Thiel is doing, and I'm not sure which one I'm doing when I find him interesting.

And what about action - are we delaying action by trying to understand the exact moral quality of them? Is it morally better or obligatory to be taking as many good actions as possible, rather than criticize people that we think are taking morally bad actions? Is the very act of taking actions more moral than not taking action? Thiel would say being able to competently take consequential actions is better than not taking an action, even if the action might not necessarily be the most morally good.

Max Weber drew this line a century ago. He contrasted an ethic of conviction, keeping your own hands clean, with an ethic of responsibility, owning the consequences of what you actually do in the world. Critics of Thiel mostly live in the first one. Thiel lives in the second, maybe too comfortably. The Bhagavad Gita, which is much older than both of them, takes a side: "Action is better than inaction." It pairs that with a condition, though: act without attachment to the fruits of your action.

This brings me to safety. I have recently been forming my own opinions in AI safety, and I found myself being stuck and not taking actions for a long time. I started to think that perhaps just taking any action is better than taking no action. This is also quite risky, as AIS right now is almost like us trying to figure out how to make flying safe, while the plane is already in the air.

There's a saying that the rules of aviation were written in blood. Every safety rule we have exists because something crashed first. Nobody wants that for AI, and that's exactly what paralyzed me: if every action might be the wrong one, then waiting feels like the safe one. But waiting is also an action. The plane keeps flying whether I touch the controls or not.

Maybe that's what Thiel's hedging is really for. It isn't indecision. It lets you act when you're uncertain. You place several bets, hold several beliefs, and let reality tell you which one was right. I don't think that makes it moral by default, and Palantir is my proof. But I'm starting to think the answer to "which action is morally right?" isn't to figure it out completely before moving. It's to act, keep watching what your actions actually do, and stay honest enough to change course. Hume would say that's the hard part, because our beliefs will always try to follow what we want.
