Polarizing ideas, the Antichrist, AI safety

November 9, 2026

P.S.: Intended to be heavily edited soon - still synthesizing my thoughts on this.

Peter Thiel is a very polarizing figure. When he was giving lectures in Rome, there was an article published about him, titled "Should we burn Peter Thiel?"

I was at Peter Thiel's Antichrist Lectures last month, and quickly saw that even just engaging with his ideas, let alone agreeing or supporting them, was very polarizing in a lot of the social circles I'm in.

Still, I got very nerdsniped by Thiel. Primarily, his epistemology and the way he deals with truth - for example, he hedges his bets by believing in multiple things at once, takes many contradicting actions knowingly, and that is very interesting to me.

This is the also someone whose famous interview question is "What important truth do very few people agree with you on?" His whole worldview is built on the idea that the consensus is usually wrong. Someone who distrusts consensus that much has a good reason not to commit to any single truth either.

I also understand the arguments against him. Palantir is building mass surveillance technologies while Thiel preaches that a totalitarian world government will lead to the arrival of Antichrist. A mass surveillance system directly aids a totalitarian regime!

My friend who went to work for Palantir and I had a lot of discussions about whether Palantir is evil. He believes Palantir is morally neutral since if Palantir didn't do what they do, someone else would do it. I don't think this is a valid reason to justify a morally neutral stance, but I also have a difficult time believing in Palantir outright being an "evil" organization.

What makes an organization evil, versus an individual?

Reinhold Niebuhr wrote a book on this, Moral Man and Immoral Society. His argument was that groups are capable of things the individuals inside them would never do alone, because no single person feels responsible for the whole. Hannah Arendt called bureaucracy "the rule of Nobody," and she wrote that "the sad truth is that most evil is done by people who never make up their minds to be good or evil." Deep stuff. I think this is causing a lot of problems, and I wonder how people can feel more responsibility for their individual actions. 

To go back to an earlier point, what's the limit of colliding what a person does and what they think? How much can a person be compartmentalized? Can you admire a dictator's art?

Orwell said "One ought to be able to hold in one's head simultaneously the two facts that Dalí is a good draughtsman and a disgusting human being." I feel I can appreciate Dalí's art while recognizing his personal failures. But I do think this is risky. This may result in us being too forgiving to people who have genuinely hurt others a lot. But can we still listen to their ideas? Should we? And I guess this is especially different if they're running an extremely consequential company, as opposed to painting.

Can you hold two beliefs at once and hedge your bets? Or is this just stupid?

F. Scott Fitzgerald thought, "The test of a first-rate intelligence is the ability to hold two opposed ideas in mind at the same time and still retain the ability to function." Keats called it negative capability, being "capable of being in uncertainties, mysteries, doubts, without any irritable reaching after fact and reason." Sort of relatedly, Robert Aumann proved that two rational people with the same priors can't agree to disagree, and I feel this is supposed to help me understand the situation better, but I can't fully say it does. Hume says "Reason is, and ought only to be the slave of the passions." Perhaps Hume here is really clocking what's beneath my fascination with Thiel's ideology - I do think part of it is beyond his reasoning and how much sense his intellectual framework makes (like I said, there are a lot of contradictions), in that, I am also fascinated on an emotional level because I want to be more like him in some ways, and this is especially difficult to discuss with people because I can't justify it unless I accept that my justification will be emotionally primed. 

And what about action - are we delaying actions by trying to understand the exact moral quality of them? Is it morally better or obligatory to be taking as many good actions as possible, rather than criticize people that we think are taking morally bad actions? Is the very act of taking actions more moral than not taking action? Thiel would say being able to competently take consequential actions is better than not taking an action, even if the action might not necessarily be the most morally good.

Max Weber has a philosophy that I can use as a framework here; he contrasts an ethic of conviction with an ethic of responsibility. The Bhagavad Gita also says "Action is better than inaction." (but act without attachment to the fruits of your action).

All of this brings me to safety in some convoluted way. I have recently been thinking more about safety, and I found myself being stuck and not taking actions for a long time. I started to think that perhaps just taking any action is better than taking no action. This is also quite risky, as AIS right now is almost like us trying to figure out how to make flying safe, while the plane is already in the air.

There's a saying that the rules of aviation were written in blood. I certainly don't want the rules of AI to be written in blood either. The plane keeps flying whether I touch the controls or not, and it becomes really easy to not touch the controls because I think I might cause things to go worse if I do. 

Perhaps that's what Thiel's hedging is really for.  It lets you act when you're uncertain. And so, I'm also personally starting to think, "which action is morally right?" isn't something to figure it out completely before starting to do stuff in the real world.

Recently I have been a lot more primed towards action, and things have been much better! I feel I am making a lot more progress than I was making for a long time. And I feel I owe that at least partly to Thiel, which is where this essay sort of comes from.
