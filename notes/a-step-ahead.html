Sunday, October 27, 2024

a step ahead

I am now an officially trained A Step Ahead volunteer! I went through an orientation session with my supervisor a few weeks ago before doing more service with A Step Ahead, so that I could be better informed and help people more. And my supervisor sent me a t-shirt afterwards.

This Saturday, I volunteered for A Step Ahead with my friend. We drove for 1.5 hours to get to our destination: Erin, Tennessee in Houston County. Erin is a quaint small city with a rich Irish heritage and a population of about 1200 residents, with an absolutely booming downtown housing 2 barbershops and churches (the last part was a joke, nothing about Erin seems to be booming, according to the residents who share their concerns about the lack of job opportunities).

My friend and I were in awe of the natural beauty we witnessed while we were in town & on our way to the town. There were so many trees and cows. It was a totally different world than what we are used to here in the city. My friend would sometimes say things like, "I think I'm going to cry. This lake is so, so beautiful". Watching the beautiful scenery on our way to the town felt like an experience I didn't deserve. I felt like I had to do... something? Or... pay for it? Or... I don't know, I had to do something to deserve a sensory experience so amazing. My friend said I should be nicer to myself (or something along those lines).

When we got to our final destination, we set up our table and tent etc. and got ready for the festival to start. To my surprise, it was an addiction recovery festival once again.

Throughout the course of the festival, we met many people and it almost felt like I watched a slice of all of their lives. That is because a lot people actually stayed at the festival for hours and talked to everyone etc. instead of just stopping by. It was a town-wide social event. It was a celebration of all the friends and family who decided to change the course of their lives by healing from addiction. At one point, one speaker even said something like "If you're recovering from addiction in Houston county, come up to the stage!" in a celebratory way, and people actually did walk to the stage. Whoa. So different from the last festival!

A lot of people shared their stories with us as well, which is similar to the previous festival. One woman who lives right up the hill told us about how her daughter was recovering from an opioid addiction, and her 2 nieces had gone through recovery programs and now had jobs. I couldn't believe how deeply this woman was affected by addiction. Many people who had gone through recovery programs told us they had hysterectomies and that they physically couldn't have children anymore — my friend and I couldn't help but wonder, "Isn't it super difficult to get hysterectomies? Do they intentionally make it easier for people who had been addicted or went to jail?.. Do they just want to erase these people from the society?" I think going to these events and interacting with people from all walks of life has been really teaching us a way of seeing things — seeing the big picture rather than just the individual pieces of information. I'm very sad that our system (likely) intentionally pushes some people outside. I'm very, very sad. I could have been one of those people if I was born in the environment they were born in. I don't have any "inherent" privilege over them, but they get pushed aside and I get to study in one of the most prestigious institutions in the world while having all of my needs provided. What if I was born in their environment? In the middle of nowhere rural Tennessee with an addicted mother and relatives, with no personal development or job opportunities? Would I have ended up in the same place?

Anyways. Another highlight from this event was the clash between us and the faith based counseling services helping with pregnancy etc. issues. Some of them didn't even want to talk to us. Some of them came to our table and told us about what they are doing. But my friend was especially receptive to the tension as she had interacted with the faith based organizations before. Apparently, they handle issues very differently than A Step Ahead does (A Step Ahead informs people about their choices, while the faith based organizations usually try to direct people in a certain direction).

Other highlights:

  • My friend and I tried a bunch of food together and it was very fun. We also got to talk about almost everything during our time together, because we were together for about 8 hours. For example, we brainstormed reasons for why addiction might be so rampant in some rural Tennessee communities, and why addiction is not as rampant in similar communities in other parts of the world. What is different about the US? What is different about the south? etc. I spent a lot of time looking at statistics and trying to understand the "addiction epidemic" and the social dynamics in the American South. In particular, looking at historical trends with relation to the American South was quite interesting. I ended up learning about migration to the US in the 19th century and history of slavery as well. I'm really trying to understand this place and what made it become the way it is, and hopefully one day I will.
  • I saw a t-shirt that said: "Everyone you meet is fighting a battle you know nothing about". Deep stuff.
  • NOBODY was looking at a screen. Everybody was either talking to other people or just calmly looking around. At some point, I actually tried to count the number of people I see in the entire place looking at a phone and I counted one person. That was it. The stark difference from the Vanderbilt campus where everyone is continuously looking at screens and never talking to each other for a sustained amount of time was intriguing. We also don't talk to each other with vulnerability and share deeply personal things most of the time unless the conversation is specifically for that, but in Erin, a lot of people shared deep struggles without us asking (haha). People were also generally very social and open, and a lot of people (women in particular) called me "sugar" or "love" when referring to me, which was interesting, because I'm usually referred to as "Um, uhh, what was your name?" at the Vanderbilt campus.
  • The main speaker talking about how we have so much more influence on the world than we think.
